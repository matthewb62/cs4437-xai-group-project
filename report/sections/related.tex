SHAP splits a single prediction into one contribution per feature, using
Shapley values from game theory \cite{lundberg2017}. Averaging the absolute
contributions over many predictions ranks the features globally. For tree
ensembles such as LightGBM \cite{ke2017}, TreeSHAP computes these values
exactly and fast enough to explain every hour of a long test period
\cite{lundberg2020}. We chose SHAP over permutation importance
\cite{breiman2001}, also covered in the module, because permutation
importance needs the true price, which a deployed model does not yet have.

Our setup follows Trebbien et al. \cite{trebbien2023}, who used
gradient-boosted trees and SHAP to explain German day-ahead prices. They
found load, wind and solar to be the main drivers, with fuel prices also
mattering. Their model analyses prices after the fact, so it uses observed
values and fuel prices, and is explained once over a fixed period. We also
follow Lago et al. \cite{lago2021} in comparing against a naive benchmark.

Concept drift is a change over time in the joint distribution of inputs
and target, which can affect the inputs, the link between inputs and
target, or both \cite{gama2014}. Many monitors watch only the inputs, and
PSI is a common example from credit scoring \cite{yurdakul2018}. A shift in
the inputs need not hurt the model, however, so such monitors can raise
false alarms \cite{mougan2025}. Explanations depend on both the data and
the model. Dem\v{s}ar and Bosni\'{c} \cite{demsar2018} detected drift in
synthetic data streams by watching a classifier's explanations change.
Lundberg et al. \cite{lundberg2020} tracked a deployed model's loss per
feature over time, but loss needs labels. Mougan et al. \cite{mougan2025}
define \emph{explanation shift} as a change in how predictions are
explained on new data compared with training data, and detect it with a
classifier on SHAP values, which they find more sensitive than checks on
the inputs alone.

We combine these strands. Unlike Trebbien et al., our main model never
sees a fuel price, so the oil shock can only reach its explanations through
the electricity data. We also follow the explanations through a real, dated
shock -- the closure of the Strait of Hormuz -- rather than one fixed
period. Unlike Mougan et al., we use a rank measure that can be read
directly, and hold it to the same warning rule as error and PSI, so the
three signals can be compared by date.
