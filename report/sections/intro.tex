Machine learning models are trained on the assumption that future data will follow the same distribution as the data used to train them. When the relationship between inputs and target changes, a problem known as concept drift, accuracy declines~\cite{mougan2025}. This is usually detected by monitoring error, yet error can only be computed once the true outcome is known, and a rise must persist before it can be separated from normal variation. Explanations offer a possible earlier signal, since they need no labels. SHAP attributes each prediction to its input features~\cite{lundberg2017}, and Mougan et al.~\cite{mougan2025} report that shifts in these attributions detect unusual model behaviour better than shifts in the inputs alone. Less is known about how explanations behave through a sudden real world event with a known onset.

On 28 February 2026 the Strait of Hormuz, which carries about a fifth of global oil consumption~\cite{eia2025}, closed. Expectations proved too severe: oil peaked a little over 50\% above its level before the conflict, where past disruptions of this size implied rises of up to 105\%~\cite{ecb2026}. If expert forecasts misjudged the shock, it is worth asking what data driven models registered, and whether their reasoning shifted before their forecasts failed.

We study German day ahead electricity prices rather than oil itself. A model of the oil price would plainly fail and reveal little, whereas electricity feels the shock one step removed, through the fuel costs of the plants that set the market price~\cite{trebbien2023}. In this paper, we ask whether the SHAP explanations of a frozen price forecasting model drift before its forecast error rises.

This paper makes two contributions. First, it presents a fortnightly monitoring study of two frozen LightGBM models~\cite{ke2017}, one without any fuel price and one given the 30 day change in Brent, comparing explanation drift, measured with weighted Kendall's tau~\cite{vigna2015}, against forecast error and input drift. Second, it shows that the early warning given by tau reflects seasonal change rather than the shock, and that a rank based measure misses a threefold growth in attribution to the previous day's price.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Related work is reviewed in Section~II, and the data, models and drift measures are described in Section~III. The results are presented and discussed in Section~IV, followed by our conclusions and the limitations of the study in Section~V.
