Concept drift, a change over time in the joint distribution of a model's inputs and its target~\cite{gama2014}, can leave a deployed model less accurate~\cite{mougan2025}. This is usually detected by monitoring error, yet error can only be computed once the true outcome is known, and a rise must persist before it can be separated from normal variation. Explanations such as SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017} need no labels, and shifts in them can detect unusual model behaviour better than shifts in the inputs alone~\cite{mougan2025}. Less is known about how explanations behave through a sudden real-world event with a known onset.

On 28 February 2026 the Strait of Hormuz, which carries about a fifth of global oil consumption~\cite{eia2025}, closed. Oil rose by less than expected. It peaked a little over 50\% above its level before the conflict, where past disruptions of this size implied rises of up to 105\%~\cite{ecb2026}. Buffers softened the rise, among them high inventories and a coordinated release of 400 million barrels of reserves~\cite{ecb2026}. A sudden shock like this, with a known start date, is a natural test of whether a model's explanations change before its error does.

We study German day-ahead electricity prices rather than oil itself. A model of the oil price would plainly fail and reveal little, whereas electricity feels the shock one step removed, through the fuel costs of the plants that set the market price~\cite{trebbien2023}. In this paper, we ask whether the SHAP explanations of a frozen price forecasting model drift before its forecast error rises.

This paper makes two contributions. First, it compares explanation drift, measured with weighted Kendall's tau~\cite{vigna2015}, against forecast error and input drift for two frozen LightGBM models~\cite{ke2017}, one of them given the oil price. Second, it shows that the early warning given by tau reflects seasonal change rather than the shock, and that a rank-based measure misses a threefold growth in attribution to the previous day's price.
