\textbf{Warning dates.} Against the October reference, $\tau_w$ first crossed
its warning line in the window starting 25 April 2026 (Table~\ref{tab:warn}),
56 days before MAE (20 June) and 112 days before mean PSI (15 August). The
oil model gave the same $\tau_w$ date, but its MAE crossed earlier (23 May),
so the lead fell to 28 days.

\textbf{Robustness.} The 25 April lead does not hold up. For the blind model,
$\tau_w$ never stayed over the line for two windows in a row, whereas MAE did
from 4 July. Against the same fortnight a year earlier, $\tau_w$ never crossed
for either model (Fig.~\ref{fig:seasonal}), and its $z$-score on 25 April was
only $+0.40$ (blind) and $+0.21$ (oil) against $+2.90$ and $+3.60$ versus
the October reference. Only the oil model's two-in-a-row result kept an early warning
(56 days).

\textbf{What moved.} MAE rose steadily after June, reaching $+6.4$ SD by
26 September, while $\tau_w$ stayed mostly within 2~SD. The explanations did
change in size. Yesterday's price (\texttt{price\_lag\_1d}) went from fourth
in the October ranking, with a mean $|$SHAP$|$ of 11.3~EUR/MWh, to first
in the last window at 35.3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}). It was in the top two in
none of the eight baseline windows and in 9 of 16 windows after the shock.
Against the same fortnights of 2025 it was top two in 3 of 16, and its
largest 2025 value was 14.7. The oil model shows the same pattern (8 of 16
after, 1 of 16 in 2025, peak 34.5).

single input reached the 0.25 PSI cut-off in the very first test window
