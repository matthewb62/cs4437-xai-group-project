The warnings both models raised on 25 April are better explained by season than by the shock. The reference ranking comes from October, when wind leads, and every baseline window falls between November and February. By late April solar led both models, at 29.5~EUR/MWh against 13.6 in October for the blind model, and it stayed on top in 12 of the 16 blind windows after the shock. The demand forecast, in the top three in all eight baseline windows but only one afterwards, likely reflects the same confound.

The larger change was in magnitude, which $\tau_w$ ignores by design. Attribution to the previous day's price tripled (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}), but as it was already fourth in October, its climb barely moved $\tau_w$.
Because the models are frozen, this growth does not mean their rules changed. SHAP values are measured relative to the model's expected output~\cite{lundberg2020}, so inputs far from the training data draw larger attributions from the same trees. With no fuel price available, yesterday's price was the blind model's only route to rising costs. Adding Brent did not change this, as the oil feature never ranked above fifth of 13. Its largest 30-day rise in training was 21.4\%, against 77.6\% after the shock, and tree models cannot extrapolate past their training range, so the shock appeared as an ordinary rise.

Explanations are better monitored beside error than in place of it. The trade-off is timeliness against reliability. Explanations are available at once but move with the season, while error is trustworthy but arrives late. SHAP also explains the model, not the market. It cannot say whether prices rose from lost supply or from expectation.
