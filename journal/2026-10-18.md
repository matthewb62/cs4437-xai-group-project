## Why
- The results have been stable since the 15 Oct rerun, so it made sense to start writing. The one check still open from that entry is whether yesterday's price was also high in the ranking in spring and summer 2025.
- Splitting every section between people means everyone contributes to the paper roughly equally, and the commit history shows it.
- Drafts by Sunday leave time to bring questions to James in class on Monday.
- We're trimming later because sections depend on each other. For example, the conclusion talks about "two windows in a row", the seasonal tau and the model being frozen, and those are only defined in `method_drift.tex`. If we cut each section on its own now, we could delete something another section relies on.
- Reasons for my `method_drift.tex` choices:
  - Two words for the same thing makes a reader think they're two different things.
  - A reader can't follow a term that hasn't been defined yet, so PSI comes first.
  - Calendar inputs are fixed by the date, so they can't drift and would only add noise to PSI.
  - I didn't want to write anything I couldn't explain if asked, and the 4-page limit doesn't leave room for how PSI works internally.
  - Every claim in the paper needs a source or our own data.
- The weighted tau and PSI comparison goes back to our [30 Sep](2026-09-30.md) entry. PSI being compared with the same fortnight last year comes from the problem we found on [5 Oct](2026-10-05.md) and the fix on [15 Oct](2026-10-15.md). Both are now in the drift measurement section.
- The numbers changed because of the 15 Oct early stopping fix. October now only checks accuracy instead of also deciding when to stop training, so it's a fairer test. We went through them so nobody copies the old slide numbers into the paper. The current values are in `results/importance_blind.csv`.
- Reasons for my `related.tex` choices:
  - Same as PSI, the 4-page limit doesn't leave room for how SHAP is calculated, and the reader only needs to know what it gives us.
  - Permutation importance needs the true price, which a deployed model doesn't have yet. That's the same problem as error being a slow warning sign, so it ties into the intro.
  - Trebbien et al. is the paper a reader would compare us to first, so it needs to be clear what we do differently.
  - Explaining why input-only monitors can give false alarms sets up why we compare PSI against tau and error.
  - Saying what each earlier paper is missing makes the gap clear before we state it, so the last paragraph doesn't come out of nowhere.
  
Reasons for Conor's `data_model.tex` choices:
- The forecasts are cited to Trebbien et al. (2023) so readers have a published source for where the forecasts come from and how reliable they are, not just a website link.
- Prices, forecasts and weather all have to line up on the same hour. Mixing time zones would shift features by an hour, or by two around daylight saving changes.
- At 12:00 on D-1, only the morning of D-1's gas generation has happened, so afternoon hours have to fall back to D-2. This tightens the plain "D-1" rule from the earlier entry, which would still have leaked a few hours of the future.
- A forecast for day D made on D-1 might not be out before the auction closes at noon. A two-day-ahead forecast definitely was, so it was really known at bidding time.
- These two hyperparameters are what a reader needs to rebuild the model, and the 4-page limit doesn't leave room for the full list.

Reasons for Conor's `conclusion.tex` choices:
- Some readers only read the abstract and conclusion, so the question and the answer both need to be there.
- Exact dates and day counts are what we actually measured. "Explanations drifted earlier" on its own would be a vague claim.
- A separate limitations paragraph matches the paper's "Conclusion & Limitations" section and keeps the findings paragraph short.
- The real numbers turn "the March jump is bigger than anything in training" (30 Sep entry) into a measured result, and recording this figure was one of the decisions in the October entry.
- Future work tied to our own results is more convincing than generic suggestions: magnitude-aware drift comes from the SHAP size finding, seasonal references from the seasonal tau check, and retraining from the frozen-model limitation.
