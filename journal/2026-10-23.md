## What We Learned
### About explanations and drift
- Explanations change for reasons other than the one you're looking for. Our biggest early result, tau warning 56 days before error, turned out to be spring solar rather than the oil shock. Without the same-fortnight-last-year check we would have reported a false finding. Any explanation drift measure needs a way to separate normal seasonal change from real change.
- The choice of drift measure decides what you can see. Weighted Kendall's tau only looks at the order of features, so it barely moved while attribution to yesterday's price tripled. The model's explanations did change after the shock, but in size, not in order.
- Explanations describe the model, not the world. Our models were frozen, so larger SHAP values came from unusual inputs going through the same trees, not from the model learning anything new. SHAP can't tell us why prices rose.
- Post-hoc explanation is a choice that needs justifying. The Week 2 material, especially Rudin, argues for interpretable-by-design models instead. We explain a black-box model because our question is about monitoring a model already in use, and stating that made our method clearer.
- A negative result is still a result. Our answer is that explanations didn't give a reliable early warning, and explaining why turned out to be the most interesting part of the paper.

### About method
- Data leakage is easy to miss. We let gas generation from the target day into the model at first, then found that even "yesterday" leaked a few hours for afternoon prices. Every feature has to be checked against when it was actually known, which for us meant noon on the day before.
- The reference you compare against matters as much as the measure. PSI flagged every window until we compared each fortnight with the same fortnight a year earlier instead of all of training.
- Using the same data for two jobs flatters the results. October decided both when to stop training and how accurate the model was, until we gave early stopping its own month.
- Tree models can't extrapolate. The oil model saw 30-day Brent rises of up to 21.4% in training against 77.6% after the shock, so it treated the shock as an ordinary rise. Giving a model a feature doesn't mean it can use it.
- Robustness checks are worth doing before presenting, not after. The two-in-a-row and seasonal checks changed our headline, and it was better to find that ourselves than in the Q&A.
- A small baseline limits how much you can trust a threshold. Eight windows is not many for a standard deviation, and we say so in the paper.

### About doing the project
- Narrow scope early. We started with electricity, traffic and flights, and dropped to one domain once we saw how much work one done properly would take.
- Data availability and licensing decide what's possible. We picked Germany over Ireland and the UK because of data coverage and clear reuse rights, and dropped the gas price because the only daily source wasn't openly licensed.
- Don't trust documentation blindly, and have a backup source. The energy-charts API went down days before the presentation, and SMARD's documentation had the wrong ID for solar and the wrong units.
- Writing the journal as we went kept the paper consistent. When numbers changed after the retrain, the journal showed which ones and why, so old slide values didn't end up in the paper.
- Splitting every section between people spread the work evenly, but it meant we had to cut and proofread together at the end so the sections fit as one paper.
